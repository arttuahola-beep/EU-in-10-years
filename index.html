France heads into a strike week, Belgium's government wobbles and Moscow rules Europe out of the Abu Dhabi talks

France's tax vote slips as unions and students prepare Thursday's national strike, Belgium's prime minister offers to resign over austerity, Poland's court fight splits president and government, Moscow rules Europe out of the 10–11 November Abu Dhabi talks, gas storage stays at a record low, and the vision is unchanged.

What changed today

  • Strengthened. France goes into its biggest strike day of the school year without a budget. On Tuesday the National Assembly put off its vote on the tax half of the 2027 budget: 758 amendments were still waiting, the vote will now come when the debate ends, which the agenda puts on Friday, and deputies have until midnight on 17 November to finish their first reading. If they reject the tax half, the whole budget falls at that stage and the government's original text goes to the Senate. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has not forced the budget through without a vote. On Monday evening, with only 58 of 577 deputies present, the National Rally and France Unbowed voted together, 30 to 28, to scrap VAT on staple goods, a measure the budget rapporteur put at about €2 billion. On Friday Insee reported that the economy shrank by 0.1 per cent in the third quarter and that inflation rose to 3.4 per cent in October, the fastest since January 2024; Finance Minister Roland Lescure said that without the budget France is "headed straight for a wall". On Tuesday students blocked 48 of 3,700 high schools and 51 of 184 higher-education institutions, the authorities say. On Thursday all the main unions, public and private, strike together for the first time since the school year began, and the yellow-vest movement has called its own "people's 5 November". An Ifop poll for LCI, taken 27–29 October, gives Marine Le Pen 35 to 37 per cent of first-round votes, well ahead of Jean-Luc Mélenchon at 17.5 to 18 and Édouard Philippe at 15. The vision says prices, schools and austerity budgets move more votes than any treaty, that protest recurs, and that a founding member run by a "Europe of nations" party is a central possibility. All of that fits. The falsifiers already cover the French result after 2027, so none is added. (nosparlementaires.fr, Cointelegraph, AFP, Insee, France 24, franceinfo, Le Parisien)
  • Strengthened. The revised energy paragraph has had its first test, and it held. The Dutch benchmark gas price passed €100 a megawatt hour on 28 October, for the first time since December 2022, and settled near €104 on Friday; it fell back to about €93 on Tuesday after Iran's president said talks with the United States had been authorised. EU storage peaked at 74.96 per cent on 27 October, the lowest autumn peak since records began in 2011, and stood at 74.6 per cent on 1 November with withdrawals under way; the average for that date is about 90 per cent. Germany is about 61 per cent full, and its failure to fill its tanks has opened a row with Italy. On Tuesday the International Energy Agency said Europe's low storage could keep world gas markets tight "well into 2027", because refilling next summer will need more gas. Euro-area inflation reached 3.8 per cent in September, with energy up 18.8 per cent. ING says that with a tight balance the weather matters more than usual and prices will stay volatile. Washington has floated sending Russian gas to Europe through Ukraine, via a US intermediary; the Commission answered that the phase-out of Russian gas and liquefied gas will be finished by the end of next year. The revised paragraph says bills jump when gas is short and that storage and demand cuts are standing policy. Nothing this week points the other way. The relief that ING and others expect from the spring of 2027 is still a forecast. (bne IntelliNews, S&P Global, ING via TMGM, GasRadar, IEA, CNBC, Kyiv Independent)

Tusk's side suspended the tribunal's president and his deputy on Friday. On Tuesday eight judges chosen by the current majority declared themselves a quorum, while Nawrocki, who refuses to swear in five of them, accused Tusk of an illegal power grab. Tusk offered him a private meeting, and the president's office says it accepts, with no date set. The row is drawing the nationalist right together before the 2027 election. The King has deferred a decision and sees him again on Friday. In Hungary the movement runs the other way. Poland shows the risk of repair without the president's signature, Belgium a second government in trouble over savings, and Hungary the speed that a supermajority allows. Vision for Tuesday 4 November 2036

Vision for 2036

This living baseline, last revised on 28 October 2026, is carried forward, not a prophecy. It pictures the European Union on 4 November 2036. The horizon moves with the publication date: always ten years on. Nothing here is the necessary path of European history.

Institutions and enlargement

The Union of 2036 rests on the same skeleton: a Commission that proposes and administers, a Council of governments, a directly elected Parliament, and a Court whose judgments bind. A treaty rewrite remains possible this decade; this text does not assume one is ratified. The tools are passerelle and bridging clauses, accession treaties with probationary safeguards, and political pledges to limit new vetoes without treaty change.

Enlargement is the largest open constitutional question. The mid-2020s queue will not move as one convoy. One or more accessions by 2036 remain plausible; so does further delay. Agriculture, cohesion money, free movement, and foreign-policy decision rules remain the brakes that matter. Any new member likely faces a decade of scrutiny, suspended rights if backsliding returns, and staged entry into sensitive policies. Unanimity on tax, foreign policy, and treaties would leave a larger Union slower; more qualified-majority voting would make it more capable, and harder on a government that loses the vote. Partial movement on both remains the central case. The open fight is whether it arrives through the package now circulating, a thinner compromise, or not at all.

The euro area is the same size or larger. Leaving the Union is not the central case. Differentiated integration — Schengen, the euro, defence projects — is how the Union lives with states that will not move together. Pre-accession participation in selected programmes is part of that differentiation, not a substitute for membership.

Economy, technology, and artificial intelligence

The single market is the Union’s main source of weight. Some income gaps have narrowed; others have stuck. Industrial policy is more explicit than before the early-2020s shocks. Subsidies and joint projects for chips, clean technology, and defence production are ordinary tools. They have not produced one industrial state. State-aid fights continue; the Commission referees.

By 2036 artificial intelligence is ordinary infrastructure in firms, offices, and campaigns. The AI Act’s outline — prohibitions, risk classes, transparency — has held, with guidance doing the daily work and enforcement uneven. Europe is a hard place to deploy high-risk systems and a secondary place to train the largest models. Assume neither a growth miracle nor a collapse of employment. The argument is who keeps the gains, which regions lose routine work, and whether schools and social insurance keep up. “Digital sovereignty” names several policies, from cloud procurement to export controls, not a completed state.

Ageing, defence, and energy all draw on the same public budgets. Joint borrowing may be repeated for a named crisis; it is not an ordinary fiscal union. Budget fights over enlargement start long before accession day.

Defence and the security posture shaped by Ukraine

Security in 2036 is still shaped by the war that began in 2022, whether that war is then active, frozen, or ended. This text does not choose an ending.

Spending and production are higher than in the early 2020s. Ammunition, air defence, and drones are industrial problems. Stockpiles are only partly rebuilt. NATO is still the alliance most members rely on. The EU does more on money, procurement, military mobility, and industry. There is no European army, only closer national forces, more joint orders, and an expectation — ahead of the equipment — that Europe can act if the United States hesitates.

Ukraine is inside that order as a member, a late-stage candidate, or a heavily armed partner. Which of those depends on the war, on reforms, and on whether governments will pay. Roadmaps raise the cost of delay; they do not decide the war or the budget. Russia is not a partner and not a closed file. Sanctions, separated energy systems, and deterrence remain the tools. A settlement is asserted more often than it is shown.

Climate and energy

The live argument in 2036 is about 2040. The 2030 targets are a mixed record. Electricity is cleaner: more wind and solar, nuclear where it was kept or rebuilt, more wires and storage. Gas is smaller and not gone, above all in industry and in cold, still weeks. Russian pipelines gave way to world LNG the Union does not control; the Gulf shock from 2026 showed new suppliers are not independence. Bills jump when gas is short; storage and demand cuts are standing policy. Grids, buildings, and factories spread the cost unevenly.

The carbon border adjustment is a normal trade tool, disputed abroad and used at home. It has not greened world industry on its own. Heat, flood, drought, and coasts take more spending than early speeches allowed. Farming and land use are among the hardest files. Climate policy and rural politics have not made peace.

Rule of law, democracy, and the home front

Some governments, members and candidates, still weaken courts, public media, or the running of elections. Cases, funding conditions, and pressure have mattered, slowly. Some backsliding has been stopped. Less has been reversed than the treaties suggest. A member that defies the Court and keeps its vote is a real risk. So is repair after an election. Safeguards aimed at new members do not by themselves repair an old member that digs in.

Campaigns run on synthetic media. Platform duties, public-service journalism, and readers who pause have done more than any ban on lies. Resilience means a government can lose, a reporter can publish, and a court can embarrass a minister.

National politics is still what most people follow. Prices, schools, hospitals, and austerity budgets move more votes than any treaty, and protest over them recurs, sometimes violently. Trust in the Union varies by country and usually trails attachment to travel, trade, the passport, and the currency. Sovereigntist parties lead or join governments in several large member states; a founding member run for a term by a party that wants a "Europe of nations" is a central possibility. Such governments stay in and bargain harder over net contributions, slow enlargement and Ukraine money, test the primacy of EU law at home, and pull migration and climate costs back to national choice. The Union absorbs this as a slower, more conditional bargain. The Parliament matters most over money or a crisis.

The United States, China, and the neighbourhood

The Atlantic link is a working alliance that quarrels over trade, technology, and burdens. Military dependence on the United States is lower than in 2022 and higher than communiqués say. A deep American withdrawal would break this baseline. So would a return to easy reliance.

China is a major market and the main systemic rival. Trade continues. De-risking has been selective: some investment screened, more care over critical inputs and sensitive technology, no general split. Full separation would land on households, and governments have not accepted that. Climate cooperation exists and is thin.

The neighbourhood is where the Union matters most and looks least tidy. Accession, association, and migration deals sit together. The United Kingdom is close and limited: useful on security and on some markets, outside the institutions. South of the Mediterranean, energy, border control, and aid buy cooperation and create dependence. Deals reduce some irregular arrivals. They do not erase the reasons people move.

In 2036 the Union is a regulatory power, a large market, and a regional security actor exposed to the world economy. It is strongest when it can condition access to its market or its funds, and weakest where force it does not control decides the result.

Society in ten points

A compact picture of European Union society at the horizon. The ten labels stay fixed. Rewrite a line only when the vision itself has a material social change — not when the day’s news only deepens an already-named path.

  1. Form of government — Multi-level Union: Commission, Council, Parliament and Court on the Lisbon skeleton; more bridging and accession safeguards than a full treaty rewrite; member states still hold the hard vetoes on tax, foreign policy and treaties where unanimity remains.
  2. Social trust — Trust in the Union varies by country and usually trails attachment to travel, trade, the passport and the currency; anger over prices, schools and hospitals lands first on national governments and feeds sovereigntist votes; national politics is still what most people follow.
  3. Type of economy — Large single market with more explicit industrial policy (chips, clean tech, defence production); ageing, defence and energy compete for the same budgets; gas bought on a world market the Union does not control, so bills still jump when supply is short; joint borrowing for named crises, not an ordinary fiscal union.
  4. Freedom of speech and press — Broad legal protection with real gaps where governments lean on public media; campaigns run on synthetic media; platform duties and public-service journalism matter more than bans on lies.
  5. Rule of law — Court judgments bind, but funding conditions and Article 7-style tools stay slow; some backsliding stopped, less reversed than the treaties suggest; a large member testing the primacy of EU law at home is a live risk; new-member probationary safeguards do not by themselves repair an old member that digs in.
  6. Political competition — Competitive elections in most member states; sovereigntist parties lead or govern in several large states, a founding member possibly among them, and bargain from inside rather than leave; Parliament matters most over money or crisis; national parties and capitals still set the daily agenda.
  7. Civil society — Dense and legal in most of the Union; thinner and riskier where governments squeeze NGOs, universities or independent media.
  8. Demography and social fabric — Ageing narrows the workforce; income gaps between regions stick even where some close; migration and neighbourhood deals shape who arrives, not only who leaves.
  9. Information and surveillance — Strong data-protection and AI Act frames with uneven enforcement; high-risk AI is hard to deploy; digital sovereignty is a bundle of procurement and export rules, not a finished state.
  10. Security apparatus in society — No European army; NATO remains the main alliance for most members; the EU does more on money, procurement, mobility and industry while national forces stay the core of coercion.
